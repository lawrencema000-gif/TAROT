I Ching Explained The I Ching is both a divination system and a philosophy of change. It encodes reality as patterns: yin (broken) and yang (solid) lines stacked into six-line figures called hexagrams. Each hexagram offers a situation, its tendency, and counsel for right timing and conduct. You consult it by forming a hexagram from the bottom up, then reading the text for the resulting figure and any changing lines (the living hinge of the reading). The I Ching is actionable, not abstract—use it to see the terrain, not to surrender choice. The Book of Changes Compiled over centuries and most associated with the Zhou dynasty, the Book of Changes has layered texts: the Judgment (core meaning), the Image (strategic posture), and line statements (specific shifts). The “Ten Wings” commentaries deepen ethics, timing, and governance, but the heart remains simple: observe change, align with it, and act in proportion. The text’s wisdom is practical. It speaks in images—wind over earth, thunder within water—not to obscure, but to compress strategy into memorable shapes. Read the Judgment to frame the situation; read the Image for comportment; read changing lines for what to do next, here and now. The 64 Hexagrams Sixty-four hexagrams arise from every possible stack of six yin/yang lines. Each is identified by number, name, trigrams (two three-line halves), and a binary structure that mirrors dynamic systems. Trigrams: Heaven, Earth, Thunder, Water, Mountain, Wind/Wood, Fire, Lake. Pairing two trigrams yields the hexagram’s “weather.” Structure: Bottom lines describe foundations; middle lines show development; upper lines point to culmination or excess. Changing lines transform a present hexagram (the “primary”) into a second (the “relating”) hexagram. Read the primary for the current stance; let the relating show background trend or outcome if you follow the counsel. I Ching Divination: Coins vs. Yarrow There are two classic casting methods: three coins or fifty yarrow stalks. Both generate each line as old yin (6), young yang (7), young yin (8), or old yang (9). Old lines (6 and 9) are “changing” and flip polarity to form the relating hexagram. Three-coin method (fast, common): Assign values: heads = 3, tails = 2. Toss three coins per line, six times from bottom to top. Totals: 6 = old yin (broken, changing), 7 = young yang (solid, stable), 8 = young yin (broken, stable), 9 = old yang (solid, changing). Probabilities favor stable lines slightly. Ideal for daily use and beginners. Yarrow stalk method (ritual, classical): A contemplative, multi-step counting process with 50 stalks that skews probabilities toward yin and fewer changing lines. It slows you down, priming attention and respect—the divination begins with the ritual itself. After casting, identify the hexagram by number and name, note changing lines, then flip those lines to see the relating hexagram. Read only the changing lines indicated. If none change, the Judgment and Image of the primary hexagram are sufficient. The King Wen Sequence The 64 hexagrams are traditionally ordered in the King Wen sequence, beginning with Qian (1, Creative) and Kun (2, Receptive). This is not random; it’s a narrative progression of arising, testing, balance, excess, and return. Paired design: Hexagrams often come in complementary pairs (e.g., 11 Peace and 12 Standstill) that illustrate flow and blockage.
